Career anxiety in undergrad students often shows up as a fear of "wasting" a degree, worrying about picking the wrong major, or panicking that everyone else already has a plan. This is extremely common and does not mean someone is behind.

Not having a clear career direction by second or third year is normal, not a red flag. Most people change their intended career path at least once after graduating, and many find their actual first job through a connection or opportunity they didn't plan for in advance.

Internship anxiety usually centers on rejection, feeling underqualified, or not knowing how to start applying. A useful reframe: internships are meant to teach skills, not require them in advance. Application rejection is a numbers game, not a verdict on someone's worth or ability.

Before a job or internship interview, physical symptoms like a racing heart, sweaty palms, or a blank mind are the body's normal stress response, not a sign of being unprepared. Preparing three or four specific stories about past experiences (using a simple situation-action-result structure) reduces blanking far more than general "just be confident" advice.

A resume doesn't need to be perfect before applying. Students often delay applications for weeks trying to make a resume "flawless," which usually costs more opportunities than a slightly imperfect resume ever would.

Comparing internship or job offers to what friends or classmates have posted online is a common anxiety trigger. Social media shows highlight reels, not full pictures — someone's one exciting internship post doesn't mean they have their entire career figured out.

Career indecision (not knowing what field to pursue) is different from career anxiety (fear and stress about the future), though they often show up together. Indecision benefits from small low-stakes exploration — a short informational chat with someone in a field, a single elective course, a small project — rather than trying to decide an entire life path in one sitting.

Imposter syndrome — the feeling of not truly deserving an opportunity or achievement — is extremely common among high-achieving students, especially around internship and job offers. It tends to fade with experience, not with more credentials.

If career anxiety comes with ongoing sleep problems, loss of appetite, or a sense of dread that doesn't lift even during breaks, that's worth mentioning to a counselor or campus career center rather than pushing through alone — career stress and general anxiety can feed into each other.

Practical next steps that genuinely help with career anxiety, in rough order of effort: talking to one person currently working in a field of interest, attending a single low-commitment career fair or info session, drafting (not perfecting) a resume, and setting one small, specific weekly goal rather than an overwhelming "figure out my whole career" goal.
