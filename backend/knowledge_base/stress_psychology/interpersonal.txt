Feeling lonely on campus, even while surrounded by people, is extremely common — especially in the first year, after a friend group changes, or after moving to a new city for school. Loneliness is about connection quality, not how many people are physically nearby.

It's common to feel like everyone else already has their close friend group figured out. In reality, most social circles at university are far less settled than they appear from the outside, and many students are quietly looking for more connection too.

Roommate conflict is one of the most common sources of interpersonal stress for undergrads. Small daily friction (noise, cleanliness, guests, schedules) usually responds better to a short, specific, calm conversation early on than to letting resentment build silently for weeks.

Homesickness doesn't only happen in the first few weeks of school — it can resurface around holidays, after a hard week, or when comparing life now to life at home. It's a normal adjustment response, not a sign someone made the wrong choice by leaving.

Making friends as an adult (including in university) usually takes repeated, low-stakes contact over time — seeing the same people in a class, club, or dorm repeatedly — rather than one big meaningful conversation. Consistency tends to matter more than intensity.

A fight or falling-out with a close friend can feel disproportionately heavy during university, partly because friend groups often double as a support system far from home. It's reasonable for that kind of conflict to affect mood and focus for longer than it might have in high school.

International or out-of-state students often face an added layer of interpersonal stress: language or cultural gaps in humor and social norms, time-zone distance from existing friends and family, and pressure to "make the most" of the experience even while adjusting. This compounding effect is real, not an overreaction.

Social anxiety in group settings (dining halls, large lectures, club meetings) often improves with smaller, lower-pressure entry points — a study group of two or three, a one-on-one coffee, a small-group project — rather than jumping straight into large social events.

Isolation that comes from actively withdrawing — skipping meals with others, avoiding group chats, declining most invitations for several weeks in a row — is worth noticing on its own, separate from ordinary introversion. A pattern of withdrawal, especially alongside low mood, is a reasonable thing to bring to a counselor.

Practical steps that genuinely help with interpersonal stress: joining one small recurring activity (a club, study group, or team) rather than several one-off events, reaching out first instead of waiting to be invited, and naming a specific need to a friend ("can we talk properly sometime this week") rather than hoping it comes up naturally.
