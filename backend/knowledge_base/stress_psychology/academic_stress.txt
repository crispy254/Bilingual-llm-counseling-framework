Academic Stress Management

Exam anxiety is a normal physiological response, not a sign of weakness or poor preparation. It often shows up as racing thoughts, difficulty sleeping the night before, or mentally blanking during the test itself.

Breaking a large workload into smaller, time-boxed sessions (e.g. 25-40 minutes of focused work followed by a short break) reduces the feeling of being overwhelmed more effectively than trying to power through in one long sitting.

Comparing your own pace or grades to classmates tends to increase stress without improving performance. Tracking personal progress against your own prior results is a more sustainable motivator.

Chronic academic stress that includes physical symptoms (persistent headaches, loss of appetite, insomnia lasting more than two weeks) or a loss of interest in things you used to enjoy is worth raising with a university counseling center, not just managing alone.

Short-term, practical coping strategies for pre-exam anxiety include: arriving early to reduce time pressure, using slow breathing (e.g. inhale 4 seconds, hold 4, exhale 6) for 1-2 minutes before the exam starts, and reframing physical arousal (fast heartbeat, sweating) as the body preparing to perform rather than a sign something is wrong.

It's common for students to feel that a single bad grade defines their entire academic trajectory. In reality, most academic programs weight many assessments over a semester, and one low score rarely has the outsized impact it feels like in the moment.
